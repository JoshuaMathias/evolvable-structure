# Adaptable structure in collaborative innovation: Cantr governance proposals

Two Cantr staff governance proposals Joshua shared on 2026-10-14 as examples of adaptable structure
applied to collaborative innovation (many volunteers proposing, checking and deciding changes to one
game). Read from the original documents on 2026-10-14. Quotes are verbatim, including typos.
Form links in the originals are left out.

- Proposal 4 - Department Decisions (a shared document, not public)
- Proposal 5 - Player Board (a shared document, not public)

Status: proposals. Neither document records whether or how each was adopted, or how it worked in
practice.

## Proposal 4: how a group of volunteers proposes, checks and decides

### One voting process for every department, with who votes as a parameter

- Evidence: "Significant decisions regarding a department’s responsibilities (including work requiring input from a second person) will be made by vote. All active members of a department may vote on that department’s decisions, and no one else. If there are multiple teams in the department, only the team(s) responsible for the decision vote."
- Why adaptable: the process is one mechanism; the electorate, the department and the team are what vary. A new department gets the process without a new rulebook.

### Why a process at all: shared responsibility and a voice for everyone

- Evidence: "Department decisions are both important and complex, and we should avoid placing such decisions on one person as much as possible. Also, this helps every department member feel part of the team and that their voice is heard."
- Why it matters: the process is not overhead around the work; it is how a group carries decisions no one person should carry, and how people come to own the result.

### Ideas judged apart from who proposed them

- Evidence: "For the Resources Department, and other departments if desired, there is an anonymous submission process (a Google Form) because some may be biased towards an idea based on the person who suggested it."
- Why it matters: the structure handles a known bias instead of relying on each person to resist it, and it is optional per department.

### A fixed rhythm so people can plan and think

- Evidence: "By having a fixed length of time for each vote, department members can plan out their time accordingly and know how long they have to make a final decision (as votes can be changed during the week). Also, by requiring a week, decisions aren’t made too quickly without giving them proper thought or without providing time for input by more people."
- Why it matters: time is the stable layer. The proposals change quickly, and the rhythm they move through does not, so volunteers with different schedules can take part.

### Defaults with stated overrides: duration, ties, early close, anonymity

- Evidence: "The vote ends in one of two ways: One week has passed. When there is a majority among members of the team or department and the chair chooses to end the vote early."
- Evidence: "If positive and negative vote counts are equal, the result is negative (no action). One may vote to abstain, which is considered a negative vote."
- Evidence: "The department chair may decide to make votes known or anonymous, either for a particular decision or for all decisions."
- Evidence: "By default, GAB votes last two weeks."
- Why adaptable: each setting has a default, and who may change it is named. The safe outcome (no action) is the default on a tie.

### A per-department list of what is voted on, open to change

- Evidence: "What needs to be voted on varies by department. The following list provides a guide, which may be adapted in the future. Where it is uncertain if something needs to be voted on, the HR department or the GAB Chair should be consulted."
- Evidence: Programming Department: "Pull requests (code changes). Voting for pull requests is generally in GitLab (approval or thumbs up) and this does not require waiting for a week." and "Bug fixes do not require a vote."
- Evidence: Players Department: "Admittance to the game does not require a vote, but is automatically accepted after one week."
- Why adaptable: the shared container (the vote) is the same, and the content (which decisions, which channel, how long) is set per department. Routine, reversible items proceed by default or skip the week.

### Escalation by significance, not by department

- Evidence: "Decisions concerning significant changes are still brought to a GAB vote, preferably after initial approval by the relevant department."
- Evidence: Game Administration Board list includes "How decisions are made.", "Decisions that are likely to have strong opposition (even by a few players).", "Existence of a department." and "The scope or responsibilities of a department."
- Why adaptable: the same process runs at two levels, and the level is chosen by how significant or contested the change is. Changing the structure itself (departments, how decisions are made) sits at the highest level.

### A second person as the verification step

- Evidence: "The minimum of two positive votes (unless the chair votes), or requiring input from a second person, helps ensure that changes to the game are properly thought-out, written, verified, and not too biased towards the desires of a single person. Also, it’s not expected that all department members closely consider all suggestions, but that at least two do, while also allowing input from everyone in the department where necessary. In other words, this voting is a formal verification process for all department work."
- Evidence: "A minimum of 1.5 positive votes is required for a suggestion to be approved. In general we hope every suggestion receives at least 2 approving votes, but in cases where there is no input from department members, approval by the chair is sufficient."
- Why adaptable: the gate is the same everywhere and scales down to a small team (the chair alone) and up to a full department.

### Revising a proposal without restarting the process

- Evidence: "If significant changes to the proposed idea or details are proposed, one may propose a new vote, without waiting for the earlier vote to be finished."
- Evidence: Resources Department votes twice: "Whether something should be implemented into the game. This vote is for a general idea, before too much effort is spent working out the details." then "Approval of the details of implementation."
- Why adaptable: an idea is approved in principle before effort goes into details, and a changed version replaces the old one instead of waiting. This is iteration built into the decision process.

### Multi-department work in a sensible order

- Evidence: "Often, decisions will be voted on by departments in an order that makes sense, to avoid a department wasting time implementing something before another department has approved it. For example, the Resources Department may vote on suggestions first and if approved, work out the details, and then the Programming Department will vote on it and then implement it if approved."

### Accountability for the owner's override

- Evidence: "The owner(s) of Cantr reserves the right to overrule a GAB decision, but to hold the owner accountable, Cantr staff may view the outcome of GAB votes and will be notified if the owner overruled the decision (this doesn’t apply to private GAB votes concerning individual persons)."
- Why adaptable: an override exists, but it is visible, so a silent exception cannot hollow out the shared process.

## Proposal 5: a Player Board that lets the wider community change the game

### Membership by contribution, through several paths with thresholds

- Evidence: "Player Board members consist of players who contributed to Cantr in the recent past."
- Evidence: "Contributions to Cantr for this purpose consist of the following (only one is required): Marketing or sharing Cantr. ... Bringing players into the game. ... Donating to Cantr. ... Active work in Cantr staff."
- Evidence: "Membership and position on the Player Board is awarded or removed on a monthly basis."
- Why adaptable: one membership rule with several entry paths, each with its own threshold for member and councilor. A new kind of contribution is one more row, not a new body.

### Three vote types on one board

- Evidence: "There are three types of votes in the Player Board: A veto on a decision made by staff. ... A vote on suggestions proposed by players. ... Election of the Player Advocate"
- Evidence: "For vetoes, there must be at least 10 Player Board members who choose to veto the decision." and "By default, no vote occurs for a suggestion approved by staff, but anyone on the player board may choose to initiate a vote."
- Why adaptable: one board and one voting tool serve vetoes, suggestions and elections, with thresholds and durations as the differences. Staff work proceeds by default, and the community can stop it.

### Silence and rejection have defined outcomes

- Evidence: "If no staff members vote on the decision two weeks after acceptance by the Player Board, the decision is accepted."
- Evidence: "If the suggestion is rejected by staff, a related alternative suggestion must be approved by staff within a month, or else the original suggestion will be approved."
- Why adaptable: no proposal stalls forever, and a rejection must produce an alternative, so the community's input keeps the work moving.

### Reasons stated in public

- Evidence: "every vote (except for elections) requires at least one councilor to publicly state why they agree to the outcome of the vote (this promotes understanding or clarity)."
- Evidence (Proposal 4): "GAB members should explain the reasoning behind their vote to the GAB, at least where the vote is negative."

### Balance between groups

- Evidence: "The Game Administration Board and staff departments vs the Player Board balance each other and ensure that one group can’t change the power of the other group or themselves without the other group’s consent."
- Evidence: "As no one can force staff to do anything, the staff members implementing the decision must accept the decision."

### Measuring what works

- Evidence: "This is a worthwhile effort, because it encourages detailed analysis of marketing efforts, such as what types of shares bring in the most players."
- Evidence (Proposal 4, Marketing): "Determining measurable goals regarding new players and retaining players."

### A standard workflow, written down

- Evidence: "The typical workflow for a suggestion to the game is the following:" (form, Trello, vote, discussion, staff, priority list) and "The typical workflow for changes proposed by staff:" (Resources vote, Player Board notified, optional veto, implementation).
- Evidence: "The Player Board is notified at \#proposed\_voting that the suggestion is being considered by the Resources Department."

## What this shows about adaptable structure

The main lesson is not reuse across groups. A process is a structure too: the stable part is how
ideas are proposed, checked and decided, and what it lets change is the game, the departments and in
the end the process itself.

1. **A process is an adaptable structure, and it can amend itself.** The top board decides "How
   decisions are made.", "Existence of a department." and "The scope or responsibilities of a
   department.", and each department's list of decisions "may be adapted in the future". The
   structure holds the rule for changing itself, so it can improve without being replaced.
   (`> Escalation by significance, not by department`; `> A per-department list of what is voted on, open to change`)
2. **The process exists so change happens well, not to slow it.** It spreads decisions no one person
   should carry, gives every member a voice, and verifies each change through a second person. It
   also keeps work moving: silence after two weeks means acceptance, and a rejection must yield an
   alternative within a month, so good ideas do not die by neglect. (`> Why a process at all: shared
   responsibility and a voice for everyone`; `> A second person as the verification step`; `> Silence
   and rejection have defined outcomes`)
3. **Commitment is staged.** The general idea is approved "before too much effort is spent working
   out the details", the details are approved next, and departments vote in an order that avoids
   wasted implementation. Cheap, reversible steps come first; costly ones wait for agreement.
   (`> Revising a proposal without restarting the process`; `> Multi-department work in a sensible order`)
4. **A stable rhythm carries fast-changing content.** A fixed week lets people plan and think, with
   an early close once a majority exists. Routine items skip the wait or pass by default, and
   significant or contested ones rise to a slower, higher level. (`> A fixed rhythm so people can plan
   and think`; `> Defaults with stated overrides: duration, ties, early close, anonymity`)
5. **Bias and power are handled by structure, not by trust.** Ideas can be submitted anonymously so
   they are judged apart from their author, boards balance each other so no group changes another's
   power alone, reasons are given in public, and the owner's override is visible. (`> Ideas judged
   apart from who proposed them`; `> Balance between groups`; `> Reasons stated in public`;
   `> Accountability for the owner's override`)
6. **Participation is open and earned.** Anyone can propose; membership comes through several kinds
   of contribution and is renewed monthly, which rewards consistency over turning up for one vote.
   Ownership of the game comes from taking part in changing it. (`> Membership by contribution,
   through several paths with thresholds`; `> Three vote types on one board`)
7. **Learning is part of the process.** Marketing sets measurable goals, and tracking shares shows
   "what types of shares bring in the most players". (`> Measuring what works`)
8. **Reuse follows from the above.** One voting mechanism serves every department and the Player
   Board, with electorate, duration and threshold as parameters. (`> One voting process for every
   department, with who votes as a parameter`)

## Limits / counter-evidence

- These are proposals. Adoption, use and outcomes are not recorded in the documents, so they show design intent, not results.
- The rules are dense: many thresholds (1.5 votes, 10 vetoes, five-times contribution, monthly membership), which is the cost of flexibility named elsewhere in the skill. The proposals do not say whether people found them easy to follow.
- The process admits it may look slow: "While this may seem like a slow process, this voting process will likely speed up discussions and decisions about suggestions". That is a prediction, not a measured result.
